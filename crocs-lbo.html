Crocs (CROX)
LBO Analysis

Could Crocs, Inc. serve as a suitable LBO target for a financial sponsor with a private equity perspective seeking a 20% equity IRR?

This project evaluates Crocs, Inc. from the perspective of a financial sponsor considering a leveraged buyout. The analysis was completed as part of the Venture Capital and Private Equity course at the DeGroote School of Business, McMaster University.

The analysis combines comparable-company valuation with a five-year LBO model to assess Crocs' attractiveness as a potential acquisition target. I evaluate the implied acquisition valuation, operating performance, capital structure, entry and exit multiples, and resulting sponsor returns under different scenarios. The objective is to determine whether Crocs can support a 20% target equity IRR and identify the key drivers and risks underlying the investment case.

Investment case

In 2024 Crocs generated approximately $4.1bn of revenue and $1.1bn of EBITDA, implying an EBITDA margin of roughly 27% on an asset-light model with capex of only 2% of sales. At the time of the proposal (November 2025), Crocs’ Market Capitalization was about $4.3bn, placing it comfortably within the project’s required size range and making it a realistic mid-cap buyout candidate. The business combines a globally recognized consumer brand with high margins, strong free-cash-flow generation (approximately $0.9bn of free cash flow in 2024), and moderate leverage of around $1.2bn of net debt. These characteristics suggest that Crocs can support a higher level of financial gearing while still deleveraging over the investment horizon, creating an attractive platform for an LBO sponsor.

Comparable company analysis

To value Crocs using market-based evidence, I conduct a relative valuation against a peer group of listed ”affordable footwear” companies with a significant presence in the Western hemisphere.

The started universe was 16 listed footwear and athleisure companies available in Refinitiv with sufficient public information. From this universe, three main filters are imposed to arrive at a focused and economically meaningful peer group. First, I restrict the comp set to companies whose equity value is broadly comparable to Crocs, eliminating very large global large caps such as Nike, Adidas and Asics, but also very small, niche, or struggling players such as Geox and Dr. Martens. Second, I only include companies where footwear is the core of the business model. This led us to exclude diversified apparel groups such as Columbia Sportswear, Levi Strauss, and Under Armour. Finally, a meaningful presence in North America and/or Europe is required, where Crocs generates the majority of its sales, and positive LTM EBITDA so that an EV/EBITDA metric is economically meaningful.

The resulting comp set balances similar product category, price point, and channel mix with reasonably tight range in size and profitability. No peer is perfectly identical to Crocs but taken together they provide a robust market benchmark for how investors value mid-cap branded footwear franchises.

The selected peer multiples can be applied to Crocs’ LTM EBITDA to translate the comparable-company analysis into an implied enterprise value range. Using the 25th percentile, median, and 75th percentile EV/EBITDA multiples produces implied enterprise values of approximately $12.0bn, $12.3bn, and $14.0bn, respectively.

This range indicates substantial potential upside relative to Crocs’ current enterprise value. The median and upper-end valuations would, however, require the market to regain confidence in Crocs’ long-term growth and brand durability. For the LBO analysis, these benchmarks provide support for an entry multiple below the peer median and an exit multiple closer to the median, allowing for potential multiple re-rating alongside operational improvements and deleveraging.

Overall, the comparable company analysis suggests that Crocs is undervalued relative to its selected peer group. Despite stronger profitability and comparable scale, Crocs trades at a significant discount to peers, creating potential valuation upside and a margin of safety for a financial sponsor.

Transaction assumptions

A $114.38 offer price per share is assumed, representing a 45% premium to Crocs’ $78.88 share price on November 20, 2025. Based on a fully diluted share count of 59.832 million, this results in a purchase equity value of $6.84bn and, after accounting for cash and debt, a purchase enterprise value of $8.30bn. With LTM EBITDA of $1.09bn, the implied entry valuation is 7.6x EV/EBITDA.

A 10% management option pool is included to align management with the sponsor and incentivize EBITDA growth, margin improvement, and debt paydown. The options have a strike price and therefore do not provide management with free value, while their exercise creates dilution for the sponsor. Management participates in the exit equity value once the exit value exceeds the purchase equity value.

Overall, the transaction assumes Crocs can be acquired at a 7.6x entry multiple, financed through a combination of debt and sponsor equity as can be seen in the sources and uses schedule below. The 45% premium represents a relatively demanding acquisition price, but is supported in the analysis by Crocs’ discount to comparable companies and provides a conservative test of the LBO investment case.

Operating model

The LBO model uses Crocs’ historical financial performance as the starting point and projects revenue, margins, working capital, capital expenditures, and financing costs over a five-year holding period. The base case assumes continued revenue growth while maintaining broadly stable operating margins, with additional upside potential from marketing efficiency, pricing discipline, and improved inventory management.

Key operating assumptions:

  • Revenue: projected using historical growth, with the base case maintaining the observed growth trajectory.
  • Gross margin: held broadly stable in the base case, with upside from pricing and scale efficiencies.
  • SG&A: projected as a percentage of revenue, allowing operating costs to grow broadly in line with the business.
  • Capex: maintained at a relatively low percentage of revenue, consistent with Crocs' asset-light business model.
  • Working capital: modeled based on historical operating relationships and expected to remain manageable as the business grows.

The resulting forecast shows EBITDA increasing from approximately $1.14bn in FY25 to $1.37bn in FY29, while cash generation remains sufficient to support substantial debt repayment over the holding period.

Cash generation is a central value-creation driver in the LBO. Free cash flow increases from approximately $387m in FY25 to $903m in FY29, allowing Crocs to repay scheduled term-loan amortization while continuing to build cash. This supports significant deleveraging over the five-year holding period.

Returns

The base case assumes a 7.0x exit EV/EBITDA multiple, below both the 7.6x entry multiple and the selected peer-group median of 11.3x. This deliberately conservative assumption avoids relying on multiple expansion as a primary source of returns.

Under these assumptions, EBITDA grows from $1.14bn in FY25 to $1.37bn in FY29, while continued free cash flow generation enables significant debt repayment. At exit, the model produces an enterprise value of approximately $9.62bn and an equity value of $5.14bn.

After accounting for management options and mezzanine participation, the sponsor achieves a 2.4x multiple of invested capital and a 19.6% IRR over the five-year holding period.

The return profile is therefore primarily driven by underlying EBITDA growth and debt paydown, rather than multiple expansion. This is important to the investment thesis: the sponsor does not need Crocs to become materially more highly valued by the market to generate an attractive return.

To assess the robustness of the investment case, the model tests the sponsor IRR across different acquisition premiums and exit EV/EBITDA multiples. The analysis shows that returns are particularly sensitive to the exit valuation. At a 45% acquisition premium, reducing the exit multiple to 5.0x lowers the IRR to 3.5%, while an increase to 9.0x raises the IRR to 30.0%. A more aggressive 65% acquisition premium still produces a 10.9% IRR at a 7.0x exit multiple.

Overall, the sensitivity analysis highlights both the downside risk of significant multiple compression and the upside potential from a favorable exit environment. Importantly, the base case does not rely on multiple expansion, as the assumed 7.0x exit multiple is below the 7.6x entry multiple.

Risks

The LBO thesis depends on Crocs maintaining strong margins and generating sufficient cash flow to service and repay its substantial debt load. The most critical risk is pressure on gross margins from higher resin and other raw-material costs, tariffs, freight expenses, or an inability to pass cost inflation on to customers. A 200–300bp decline in gross margin would materially reduce EBITDA and free cash flow, slowing deleveraging and putting downward pressure on sponsor returns.

The investment is also exposed to changes in consumer preferences and pricing. Weaker demand for Crocs’ core products or underperformance of HEYDUDE could force the company to increase promotional activity, reducing realized prices and weakening the operating improvements assumed in the base case.

In addition, sponsor returns remain sensitive to the valuation at exit. A significant contraction in the EV/EBITDA multiple would materially reduce returns, while the transaction’s substantial debt financing increases exposure to weaker operating performance, higher financing costs, and a broader economic slowdown. Ultimately, these risks are interconnected: weaker margins or demand would reduce EBITDA and cash generation, slowing debt repayment and reducing the equity value available to the sponsor at exit.
